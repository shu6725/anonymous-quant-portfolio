> **Crypto regulation series #1 — Overview**  
> This note reflects primary materials available on 10 September 2026. It is not investment, tax or legal advice. Check the original source, effective date, product scope and treatment in the relevant jurisdiction before acting.

## Summary: one asset, three regulatory answers

Bitcoin is one network, but the legal market that a participant reaches is not one market. The EU has chosen to bring crypto-asset services into a common rulebook. The US is working through the boundaries of existing securities and commodities law using agency interpretation and product-level review. Japan is moving crypto assets beyond a payments-only seat and toward the architecture of investment-market regulation.

This is not a contest over which regulator understands the technology best. It is a difference in the order of risks that each jurisdiction seeks to control.

- **The EU** seeks to standardise authorisation, disclosure and access to the market.
- **The US** applies established legal concepts to the asset, the transaction and the product, and develops the boundary through interpretation and review.
- **Japan** connects investor protection, disclosure and market integrity to the legal position of crypto assets as investment products.

For a trader, “regulated” is not a binary state. The operative question is which legal entity can offer which product to which customer, with what collateral, reporting and leverage conditions. Those choices move the location of liquidity before they move a price.

## Regional deep dives

The following notes unpack the three systems separately. Each starts from a market-structure question instead of treating “crypto” as one homogeneous product category.

## 1. EU: standardise the entrance to the market

The starting point in the EU is the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, or MiCA. It applies from 30 December 2024 in general, while the provisions on asset-referenced tokens and e-money tokens applied from 30 June 2024. [Article 149 of MiCA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A02023R1114-20240109) shows that the framework is not only about issuers: it treats crypto-asset service providers, or CASPs, as a combined authorisation, organisational and conduct-regulation unit.

Existing providers have a transitional regime under [Article 143](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A02023R1114-20240109). The default endpoint is 1 July 2026 or the earlier grant or refusal of authorisation, but Member States may opt out or shorten the transition. A pan-European brand is therefore not enough information. The authorised entity, home state, service scope and local transition arrangement all matter.

MiCA is also not the whole crypto market. ESMA’s guidance on the qualification of crypto assets as financial instruments directs market participants to assess crypto-asset derivatives and tokenised perpetual futures against the MiFID II financial-instrument categories. [ESMA’s final guidelines](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2024-12/ESMA75453128700-1323_Final_Report_Guidelines_on_the_conditions_and_criteria_for_the_qualification_of_CAs_as_FIs.pdf) are a reminder that a spot-service framework and a derivatives framework cannot be collapsed into one word, “crypto.”

**The EU design can be summarised as: align the entrance, then make activity through that entrance visible.**

## 2. US: operate the boundary through interpretation and product review

The distinctive US feature is that a single comprehensive market-structure statute did not first enclose the entire market. In July 2025, the House passed the Digital Asset Market CLARITY Act by 294 to 134, but a House vote is a step in the legislative process, not proof that a comprehensive framework is complete. [House Clerk roll call](https://clerk.house.gov/Votes/2025199)

That does not mean nothing is happening while legislation evolves. The GENIUS Act, enacted in July 2025, established a federal framework for payment stablecoins that includes permitted issuers, liquid-asset reserves and monthly disclosures. [Enactment announcement](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefings-statements/2025/07/the-president-signed-into-law-s-1582/) [Fact sheet](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2025/07/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-signs-genius-act-into-law/)

On 17 March 2026, the SEC and CFTC issued a joint interpretation concerning the application of federal securities laws to certain crypto assets and transactions. The SEC releases are 33-11412 and 34-105020. The interpretation makes it important to distinguish the asset itself from the way it is offered or transacted. [SEC interpretation](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/interp/2026/33-11412.pdf) [SEC announcement](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2026-30-sec-clarifies-application-federal-securities-laws-crypto-assets)

In May 2026, the CFTC adopted a policy on listing perpetual contracts and approved a Bitcoin spot-price-referenced perpetual contract on KalshiEX as a futures contract on a DCM. [CFTC policy announcement](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/pr-9242-26) [KalshiEX approval](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9240-26)

**The US design can be summarised as: do not draw the whole boundary at once; operate it inside existing law.**

## 3. Japan: move the legal seat from payments toward investment markets

Japan’s policy discussion starts from the growth of crypto assets as investment objects. The Financial Services Agency’s crypto-asset working group published its report in December 2025, setting out a direction to connect the framework to the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act rather than relying only on a payments-law framing. [Working group report](https://www.fsa.go.jp/singi/singi_kinyu/tosin/20251210/01.pdf) [Regulatory overview](https://www.fsa.go.jp/singi/singi_kinyu/tosin/20251210/02.pdf)

On 23 July 2026, the amendment to the Financial Instruments and Exchange Act and the Payment Services Act was promulgated as Act No. 64. The legislative record provides that the effective date is to be set by Cabinet Order within a period not exceeding one year after promulgation. [Legislative history](https://www.shugiin.go.jp/Internet/itdb_gian.nsf/html/gian/keika/1DE278A.htm)

Tax timing has the same dependency. The FY2026 tax-reform outline provides for separate taxation at 20% and transaction reporting for certain specified crypto assets, with application tied to 1 January of the year following the year in which the FIEA amendment takes effect. [Ministry of Finance outline](https://www.mof.go.jp/tax_policy/tax_reform/outline/fy2026/08taikou_01.htm)

**Japan’s design can be summarised as: connect protection, disclosure and market integrity to the legal position of a crypto asset as an investment product.**

## What differs at the spot and service layer

| Lens | EU | United States | Japan |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starting idea | CASP authorisation and a common rulebook | Existing securities and commodities law applied through interpretation | Connect crypto assets to an investment-market framework |
| Unit to inspect | Authorised entity, home state, notification and service scope | Registered entity, product, transaction and agency jurisdiction | Registration, conduct requirements, disclosure and market-integrity implementation |
| Current timing | MiCA applies; transition normally ends by 1 July 2026 | GENIUS Act, agency interpretation and product review operate in parallel | Amendment promulgated; effective date is still set by order |
| Transparency path | DAC8 reporting framework | Legal analysis depends on asset and transaction design | Transaction reporting and tax application are linked |

DAC8 has applied from 1 January 2026 and establishes collection and annual reporting of transaction information concerning EU residents by reporting crypto-asset service providers. [European Commission DAC8 overview](https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/taxation/tax-transparency-cooperation/administrative-co-operation-and-mutual-assistance/directive-administrative-cooperation-dac/dac8_en) It does not itself prohibit a trade; it embeds tax transparency in market infrastructure.

## Derivatives are a different layer again

Putting spot and derivatives in one comparison table too early makes regulation analysis imprecise. In the EU, a product that looks crypto-native can still require a financial-instrument analysis under MiFID II. In the US, a Bitcoin-referenced perpetual has been approved on a DCM, while other underlyings and designs retain product-specific review. In Japan, the relevant operational effect still needs to be tracked through subordinate rules and supervisory practice.

Three practical lessons follow.

1. **Do not treat a perpetual as one generic crypto product.** Separate underlying, venue, counterparty, clearing and customer category.
2. **Liquidity attaches to legal entities, not a logo.** The same brand can offer different collateral, order books and products through different regional entities.
3. **A regulatory event is not only a price event.** Product availability, open-interest location, stablecoin pairs, onboarding and reporting cost often move first.

## The timing gap creates strategy

The EU has placed an endpoint on transition at the market entrance. The US is moving parts of the market through interpretation and product review while broader legislation remains in motion. Japan is in a preparation period in which the effective date and tax start are linked.

These are different speeds at which each market is approaching its regulatory perimeter. EU firms reorganise products and customers around authorised entities. US firms expand what a registered entity can offer while following interpretation and product-review updates. Japanese firms prepare for the combined implementation of effective-date, reporting and tax changes.

## Conclusion: regulation changes the market address before it explains the price

Watching whether BTC rises or falls on the day of a regulatory announcement sees only half the subject. What changes first is who can open an account where, which collateral can be used, which contract can be offered by which entity and which transactions must be reported. Prices form later, on the liquidity that has moved.
